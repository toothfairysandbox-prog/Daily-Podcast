The United States has officially lost its measles elimination status. The Pan American Health Organization made the call because a single measles strain has now circulated continuously in the country for more than a year — the technical threshold for moving from "eliminated" to "endemic." That status held since 2000. The cause isn't mysterious: record case counts this year, falling vaccination rates in multiple states, and outbreaks that never got fully contained before spreading again. This is a policy and public-health failure showing up in epidemiological bookkeeping, not a sudden new threat to any one person, but it does mean the virus now has a foothold here the way it does in places that never eliminated it. Expect this to show up as a talking point in vaccine policy fights for a while.

Two more worth flagging. A Finnish study tracked children for eight years and found that kids who spent more time on screens tended to have better cognitive processing as teenagers — which cuts against the usual assumption that more screen time is simply bad. The researchers' read is that what kids are doing on screens likely matters more than total hours, and this is one long cohort study, not a green light to stop thinking about content. Separately, new brain-imaging work finds autistic and non-autistic brains look similar on average — but individuals with autism vary far more from each other, especially in regions tied to social processing. That variability is likely why past studies using group averages kept missing real differences.

The one to be careful with: coverage of that autism finding. It's genuinely interesting, but it's descriptive brain-imaging data, not a new test or treatment, and "more individual variability" doesn't yet translate into practical guidance for anyone.

Worth watching: flu activity is climbing ahead of Thanksgiving travel, and FDA's rejection letter for a closely watched lung cancer drug from Summit Therapeutics was just made public, laying out exactly what regulators want fixed.
