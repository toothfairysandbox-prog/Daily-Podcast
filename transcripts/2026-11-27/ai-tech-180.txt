Nvidia has told manufacturing partners to pause server shipments to Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, and Indonesia. The official reason is Washington tightening the screws to keep AI chips from reaching China through third countries — these four have become known transshipment points. But the timing is awkward, because Nvidia is also in the middle of a genuine scandal: a former Megaspeed executive, Alice Huang, who had an affair with CEO Jensen Huang, is now the subject of unproven allegations that she's a Chinese intelligence asset. Nothing's been substantiated, but the overlap of a personal scandal with a sudden chip clampdown is exactly the kind of thing that makes export-control enforcement look political rather than procedural. For anyone building on Nvidia hardware in Southeast Asia, the practical effect is the same either way: deployment timelines in that region just got less predictable, and it's a reminder that the chip supply chain runs through geopolitics first and spreadsheets second.

Elsewhere, Anthropic went public this week, and TechCrunch made the sharper point that the same day, Grand Theft Auto six launched as one of the biggest entertainment releases ever — a reminder that painstaking, inefficient human craft still draws people in a way AI efficiency pitches don't. Worth sitting with if you're tired of hearing "efficiency" used as a synonym for "better." Separately, an AI agent completed what's being called the UK's first cardless purchase, buying a Papa John's pizza on its own through a bank-to-bank payment rail — a small but real milestone for agents actually handling money, not just information. And a new paper claims a deterministic subexponential algorithm for the discrete logarithm problem, which if it holds up matters a lot for cryptography researchers, though it's not an immediate threat to anything deployed.

The US Air Force's cloud computing platform got hacked — details are thin, but it's the kind of breach that deserves more follow-up than it's getting right now. And the indie browser Ladybird, built from scratch without a Chromium or Firefox engine underneath, entered alpha testing, a genuinely rare thing in a browser market with basically two engines left.
