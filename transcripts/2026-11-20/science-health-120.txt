Researchers at U.C. Berkeley have identified a compound that, in mice, burns fat by boosting energy expenditure up to eighteen percent, rather than suppressing appetite the way Ozempic or Wegovy do. It also improved blood sugar control, cut liver fat, and spared muscle, something current weight-loss drugs struggle with. It's early, mouse-only work; whether it holds up in humans is a different question entirely.

Relatedly, a study in Nature found that resistance exercise helps middle-aged and older adults preserve muscle mass and strength while taking weight-loss drugs, a practical finding for something already widely prescribed.

Separately, researchers studying CTE, the degenerative brain disease linked to repeated head impacts, drew blood from twenty-one football players while alive and compared it to brain tissue donated after death. Small sample, early stage, but a real step toward a blood test for a disease now only diagnosable postmortem.

And a UK study tested two hundred twelve plant-based foods and drinks, oat milk, almond milk, meat substitutes, and found every one contained at least one fungal mycotoxin. Before you panic: these toxins are common in grains broadly, and the study doesn't establish the levels found were actually harmful. It's a contamination survey, not a danger verdict.
