Researchers at U.C. Berkeley have identified a compound that burns fat differently than drugs like Ozempic or Wegovy. Instead of suppressing appetite, it appears to push the body to burn more energy. In mice, it boosted energy expenditure by up to eighteen percent, improved blood sugar control, reduced liver fat, and caused weight loss without the muscle loss that usually comes with it. That muscle-sparing part matters, because muscle loss is one of the real downsides of current weight-loss drugs. But this is a mouse study. Nothing here tells you how it behaves in a person, and the jump from mouse metabolism to human metabolism has killed a lot of promising compounds before. Worth tracking, not worth excitement yet.

On that same theme, a new look published in Nature found that resistance exercise, lifting weights, can help middle-aged and older people hang onto muscle mass and strength while on weight-loss drugs. That's a much more immediately useful finding, since it's about something people can actually act on now, with therapies already in wide use.

Separately, researchers studying chronic traumatic encephalopathy looked at blood drawn from twenty-one football players while they were alive, later matched against brain tissue donated after death. It's early, exploratory work, a small sample, but it's a real step toward a blood test for a disease that right now can only be diagnosed postmortem.

And a study out of the UK tested two hundred twelve plant-based foods, meat alternatives, oat milks, almond and soy milks, and found every single one contained at least one fungal toxin called a mycotoxin. That sounds alarming, but these toxins are extremely common in grains generally, the levels weren't specified as dangerous, and the study doesn't tell you anything about actual health risk at the amounts found. It's a finding about contamination prevalence, not about harm.

Also notable: a fresh look at how language is actually produced in the brain challenges a model that's stood for a hundred fifty years. And the Taung Child fossil, three million years old, is forcing a rethink of how many distinct branches once made up our ancestry.
