Anthropic went public this week in the biggest IPO on record, pricing at eighty three dollars a share and pulling in nearly a hundred and five billion dollars for the company. Its seven founders personally cashed out close to two billion dollars between them, about two hundred and eighty million each. But the listing didn't do what IPOs are supposed to do for a sector's confidence. Global markets slumped anyway, extending an AI sell-off that the Guardian reports Anthropic's debut failed to stop. The split matters: investors clearly still want in on frontier AI, but they're pricing in real doubt about whether the spending behind it pays off.

Nvidia's week shows why. The stock is down nearly forty percent from its October sixth peak, interim chief Colette Kress spent the earnings call reassuring investors, and California's attorney general has now subpoenaed the company over ousted CEO Jensen Huang's ties to China, calling the conduct "bright red flags" for misleading investors. TechCrunch also has new reporting on Huang's management style, described as built on fear, complicating Elon Musk's claim that employees "love" him.

Apple is reportedly building its own AI agent to compete with Meta's Muse, Anthropic's Claude, and OpenAI's Dots — hiring an engineer to lead an assistant that can browse, work across apps, and write code for you. That's a notable shift for a company that's mostly sat out the agent race.

Signal is pushing back the other way, now blocking Gemini and Siri from reading on-screen content, saying agentic AI "undermines" its privacy guarantee. Worth watching as more operating systems build in screen-reading agents by default.

A post on an AI-designed CPU architecture, called Frostbite, climbed to the top of Hacker News today — interesting, but it's one blog post with no independent verification yet, not an industry shift. Nvidia's next earnings call and any formal findings from California's subpoena are the things to actually watch next.
