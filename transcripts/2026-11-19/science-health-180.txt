A cardiologist writing in STAT is publicly puzzled by the new STREAM trial, which is getting blockbuster coverage for finding that older patients could safely stop taking statins. His point: the trial's actual findings don't support the sweeping "you can quit your statin" headlines it's generating. This is a case where the coverage is well ahead of the evidence. Nothing here is a reason to change what anyone is taking — that's a conversation for a doctor, with your own cardiovascular risk in the room, not a headline.

On free will: new brain research finds that when people make free choices versus when they're forced into a decision, the underlying neural activity leading up to the moment looks surprisingly similar, building gradually before the action happens. That's a genuine challenge to some simple pictures of free will, but the researchers are careful to say personal preference and experience still shape what gets chosen. It's a single study in an area that's been contentious for decades — worth watching for replication, not worth rewriting your sense of agency over.

Food safety: researchers tested 212 plant-based products sold in the UK — meat alternatives, oat milk, almond milk, soy milk — and found every one contained at least one mycotoxin, a naturally occurring fungal toxin that contaminates grains. That sounds alarming, but these compounds turn up widely in the food supply generally, and the study doesn't tell us yet whether the levels found pose a real health risk. Worth a proper risk assessment before anyone changes their diet over it.

And in mice, researchers found that excessive training without recovery left dopamine elevated in a brain region tied to memory and emotion, still elevated 48 hours later — a possible clue to why overtrained athletes get insomnia and anxiety. It's animal data, small and early, but it maps onto something athletes have long described anecdotally.

Worth watching: STREAM trial's full data and how cardiology societies respond, since guideline bodies will have to weigh in on whether this changes anything for older patients already on statins.
