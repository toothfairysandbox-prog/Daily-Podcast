The FDA approved a new breast cancer drug from Roche yesterday, giving some patients another treatment option and giving the company what it's calling a potential blockbuster. It's a meaningful approval for patients, though as with any new cancer drug, the real test is how it performs once it's prescribed broadly rather than in trial populations.

A few other things worth knowing. Scientists have linked Neanderthal DNA to a lower risk of schizophrenia in modern humans, part of a growing body of work on how interbreeding thousands of years ago still shapes our genetics today. This is an association from genetic analysis, not a mechanism anyone's nailed down yet, but it adds to a real and growing picture of ancient DNA's influence on mental health. Separately, researchers reported that hormone therapy after menopause may improve cognition, though the benefit appears to depend heavily on which hormone and on a person's genetics. That caveat matters: this is not a blanket case for hormone therapy, it's a reason the next trials need to stratify by genotype. And a new review ties biological sex, genetics and menopause to differences in Alzheimer's risk and chronic pain conditions like fibromyalgia, including surprises in immune activity and gut-brain signaling. It's a framework-building paper more than a single finding, pointing toward more personalized treatment down the road rather than delivering one now.

On the policy side, the FDA created a new regulatory category for the supplement NAC, closing a legal gray area it's occupied for years. And NIAID's acting director, John Powers the Third, has been made permanent, worth noting given the agency's role in infectious disease research funding.

If you're hearing that hormone therapy news as a straightforward "take this for your memory," slow down. The effect was genotype-dependent and formulation-dependent in the actual research, which is a long way from a doctor's recommendation for the general population.

Watch for how Roche's drug gets priced and adopted over the next few months, and for whether the Alzheimer's sex-difference research starts producing testable treatment trials rather than just better maps of the problem.
