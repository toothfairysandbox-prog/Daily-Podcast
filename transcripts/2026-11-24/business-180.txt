Jensen Huang is back running Nvidia, eleven days after his ouster over an alleged affair with a Chinese spy, and the market's verdict was immediate: almost a trillion dollars added to Nvidia's value, the Nasdaq up six and a half percent. But the return came with pressure attached. Trump said publicly he'd "convinced" Nvidia's board to reinstate Huang, drawing backlash over a sitting president leaning on a corporate board. And Wall Street analysts are split — some call the board's move pragmatic given how irreplaceable Huang is to the AI trade, others say his judgment is still in question and the board now owes the market a real succession plan, something it notably didn't have before any of this happened. Watch CFO Colette Kress too; a reported SEC accounting probe is adding to the scrutiny on Nvidia's financial oversight, not just its leadership.

Elsewhere, cybersecurity consolidated in a big way: CrowdStrike is buying Zscaler in a forty-four billion dollar all-stock deal, betting that AI-driven threats make unifying endpoint and network defense worth the premium. Sanofi struck the year's largest pharma deal, forty-one billion dollars for Insmed, restocking its drug pipeline. And Best Buy posted its best comparable sales growth in five years, six point one percent, beating estimates and raising full-year guidance — a real signal that electronics spending is holding up better than feared heading into the holidays.

The pattern underneath all this: corporate governance is getting tested by concentration risk. One person's conduct can move a trillion dollars at Nvidia; one company's AI bet reshapes cybersecurity spending. If boards start publicly distancing from political pressure, or Nvidia names a real successor plan, that would ease my worry here.
