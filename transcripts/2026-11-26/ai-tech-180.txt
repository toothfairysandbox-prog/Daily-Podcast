S&P is considering downgrading Nvidia's credit rating, and the reasoning matters more than the number itself. The agency is flagging governance and what it calls "key man" risk — meaning Nvidia's fortunes are seen as too tied to Jensen Huang personally. That concern got sharper this week after reporting that Huang himself broke the company's own code of conduct, the kind of thing that normally triggers consequences for anyone else on staff. For a company that's become the backbone valuation of the entire AI boom, even a one-notch downgrade to AA-minus is a signal that rating agencies are starting to price in concentration risk at the top of the industry, not just chip demand.

A few other things worth knowing. OpenAI is merging its Chat and Work products into a single usage meter, which means heavy ChatGPT users should expect to hit limits sooner than they're used to — a quiet sign that compute costs are getting rationed even at the top of the market. The FCC approved SpaceX's plan to put AI data centers into orbit, waving off concerns about interference with scientific research because it decided those concerns sit outside its authority — worth watching for the precedent, not the hardware. And Anthropic's Dario Amodei pushed back hard on accusations that he's comfortable ceding human control to AI, even as his own company and its rivals keep automating their own research pipelines.

The Character.AI story is getting a lot of play, but the headline — 42 state attorneys general trying to block a chat-data sale — is really a bankruptcy footnote. Character.AI is in Chapter 11, and this is standard procedure, consumer protection catching up to distressed-asset sales, applied here to twenty-one million people's private conversations. The real story is that company's collapse, not emergency heroics from state AGs.

Watch for how S&P actually rules on Nvidia's rating — that decision will tell you whether Wall Street thinks AI's biggest supplier has a succession problem.
