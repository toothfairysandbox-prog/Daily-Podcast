The Army went ahead with its plan to destroy five tons of lead azide in Iowa, a chemical that becomes explosively unstable with age and temperature swings. Officials are calling the controlled blast a success and say no lead has turned up in soil testing around the site so far. That's the claim, not the final word — this kind of contamination testing takes time, and a public health specialist consulted by NPR was clear that air and soil monitoring needs to continue for weeks, not days, before anyone declares the area clean. Worth watching rather than worth relaxing about yet.

On the drug front, the FDA approved a new treatment from Cogent Biosciences for a rare stomach cancer, used alongside Sutent, the much older Pfizer drug it's paired with. That's a real, if narrow, win — rare cancers get thin pipelines, and combination approvals like this usually mean a meaningful subset of patients now has an option that didn't exist last week.

There's also a tidy psychology finding worth knowing: in a new study, men and women tested about equally capable at multitasking overall, but men were more than twice as likely to simply tune out a conversation while juggling other tasks. That gap, not raw ability, may be what's driving the stereotype that women are better jugglers. It's one study's framing of a well-worn claim, so treat it as a new angle, not a final answer.

Now the one getting stretched: a widely shared piece on eating-time and Thanksgiving health lands in "what time should you eat" territory based on research suggesting timing might matter roughly as much as content. That's a real area of study, but applying it to a single holiday meal is doing more work than the evidence supports — nobody's metabolism is getting fixed by moving dinner an hour earlier.

What to watch: soil and air testing around the Iowa site over the coming weeks will tell you whether "no lead detected" holds up, and that's the number that actually matters here, not Wednesday's press conference.
