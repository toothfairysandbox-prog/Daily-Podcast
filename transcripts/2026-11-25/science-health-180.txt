The Army is detonating a cache of degraded lead azide in Iowa this morning, and health secretary Robert F. Kennedy Junior has weighed in as experts warn of real health consequences. Lead azide is a toxic explosive compound, and the concern is fallout exposure for nearby residents. The Iowa Poison Control Center's medical director has public guidance out today on how people nearby can protect themselves — this is an unfolding situation, not a one-off scare, and worth tracking through the day.

On firmer scientific ground: researchers have identified a new class of broadly neutralizing antibodies against HIV, potent enough across diverse strains that the field is newly optimistic a vaccine is within reach. That's a genuine shift in tone after decades of disappointment, though there are still major hurdles between promising antibodies and a deployable shot — this is early-stage immunology, not a product.

Separately, a study of more than ten thousand people found that gut bacteria accumulate specific mutations as we age, though few of those mutations were tied to disease. It's a window into how our microbiomes evolve with us, not evidence those changes are harming us.

And in England, heat-linked deaths hit a record five thousand two hundred this year — double the previous record year, according to the UK Health Security Agency. That's a stark marker of how heat is now functioning as a public health threat in a country not built for it.

On the overhyped side: a new nasal treatment restored youthful memory in aging mice and blunted Alzheimer's-like decline, using mRNA-style technology to boost a brain-protecting protein. It's a clean result in mice. Mouse brains are not human brains, and nose-drop neuroscience has a long history of not surviving the jump to people — worth watching, not worth getting your hopes up over yet.

Meanwhile, a quieter but real story: doctors are fighting disinformation about vitamin K shots for newborns, a routine injection that prevents a rare but genuinely devastating bleeding disorder, and parents refusing it are landing babies in intensive care.
